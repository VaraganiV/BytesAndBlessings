## The Day a King's Court Laughed

Thousands of years ago, in the court of King Janaka of Mithila, the most brilliant scholars of the age had gathered. A great debate was underway — the kind where reputations were made and destroyed.

Then a boy walked in.

His name was Ashtavakra. He was twelve years old. And his body was bent in eight places — crooked, twisted, broken-looking.

The moment the scholars saw him, they burst into laughter.

*Look at this creature. What could he possibly teach us?*

Ashtavakra stood still. He let them laugh.

Then he spoke.

> "I did not know this was an assembly of cobblers."

Silence.

King Janaka leaned forward. "What do you mean?"

Ashtavakra replied: *"A cobbler judges by the skin. I thought this was an assembly of seekers who look beyond the surface. I was mistaken."*

In one sentence, the crooked boy had exposed the crooked minds.

The scholars saw a deformed body. Ashtavakra saw scholars who couldn't see.

Same room. Same moment. Two completely different realities.

---

## The Difference

This is the war between perception and perspective.

**Perception** is what your senses collect. The raw data. The scholars perceived Ashtavakra's bent body. Their eyes worked perfectly.

**Perspective** is the meaning you assign. The scholars' perspective said: Broken body = broken mind. Ugly = unworthy.

Their perception was accurate. Their perspective was the lie.

**Perception is what enters your eyes. Perspective is what exits your mind.**

The scholars' eyes took in Ashtavakra exactly as he was. Their minds released the verdict: "Not worth listening to."

And here's the brutal truth — your mind is always releasing verdicts. You're never just seeing. You're always interpreting, judging, concluding.

The question is: *Who trained your mind to conclude what it concludes?*

---

## The Mirror

Before you judge those scholars, look closer.

You are them. Every single day.

The colleague with the thick accent — what did you assume about their competence before they even finished speaking?

The person who didn't text back — what story did you tell yourself? That they're ignoring you? That you don't matter? Based on what? Silence?

The family member who voted differently — did you see a human with different experiences, or did you see an enemy?

Your eyes work fine. It's your conclusions that are broken.

**You don't see people. You see your assumptions dressed up as people.**

---

## The Uncomfortable Truth

Here's what no one will tell you:

**You're addicted to your perspective.**

Not because it's true. Because it's comfortable.

Your judgments make the world feel manageable. This person is good. That one is bad. This one is worth my time. That one isn't. Neat little boxes. Everything sorted.

But here's what's actually happening:

Every snap judgment is a door closing. Every assumption is a wall going up. And day by day, you've bricked yourself into a tiny room — convinced you're seeing the whole world through the window, when you're actually staring at a wall you painted yourself.

That colleague who's "out to get you"? They don't think about you at all. You invented an enemy.

That friend who "abandoned" you? They got overwhelmed with their own life. You wrote a betrayal story because anger feels better than feeling forgotten.

That person you dismissed in three seconds? They might have been the most important conversation of your year. You'll never know. Your perspective decided before your perception even finished.

**Most of your suffering isn't happening to you. It's being authored by you.**

And the hardest part? You'll defend your perspective to the death — because changing it means admitting you were wrong. It means grief for all the people you misjudged. All the doors you closed. All the years you spent fighting enemies that didn't exist.

So you keep the filter on.

And you call it "seeing clearly."

---

## How the Prison Breaks

Back to King Janaka's court.

After Ashtavakra's one-line response, everything shifted. Janaka, unlike his scholars, didn't defend his perception. He got curious.

He invited Ashtavakra to speak.

What followed became the **Ashtavakra Gita** — one of the most profound dialogues on consciousness and reality in all of Hindu philosophy. A king learned liberation from a twelve-year-old boy he could have dismissed with a glance.

What changed?

Not the facts. Ashtavakra's body was still crooked. The scholars were still decorated. The king was still powerful.

**What changed was distance.**

Janaka stepped back from his own perspective. He saw that his perception (crooked boy) had been hijacked by his perspective (therefore worthless). And in that tiny gap of awareness, he made a different choice.

This is exactly what Krishna does for Arjuna in the **Bhagavad Gita**. Arjuna perceives the battlefield and sees tragedy — his teachers, his cousins, his grandfather. His perspective says: *I cannot fight. This is only grief.*

Krishna doesn't change a single fact. He gives Arjuna distance from his own lens. He shows him a wider perspective — the eternal nature of the soul, the duty that transcends personal attachment.

Same war. New eyes.

The battlefield didn't change. Arjuna did.

---

## The Practice

You can't stop perceiving. Your senses will keep doing their job.

But you can catch your perspective in the act.

Next time you feel a strong reaction — anger, judgment, hurt — pause.

Ask two questions:

1. **"What did I actually perceive?"** (Just facts. No story.)
2. **"What meaning did I add?"** (The story. The assumption. The conclusion.)

You'll find that most of your suffering lives in the second answer.

The email from your boss didn't ruin your evening. Your interpretation did.

The comment from your partner wasn't an attack. Your perspective made it one.

The stranger who didn't smile isn't rude. You don't know their world.

---

## The Landing

The scholars laughed at Ashtavakra and thought they were seeing clearly.

They weren't.

They were looking at a crooked boy. But the only thing crooked in that room was their seeing.

I think about this more than I should.

How many times I've looked at someone and decided who they were in seconds. How many conversations I never had. How many people I reduced to a label and filed away.

I wasn't seeing them.

I was seeing my own assumptions, walking around in their body.

**The world doesn't show you what's there. It shows you what you're ready to see.**

Ashtavakra's body was bent in eight places.

The scholars' minds were bent in more.

*Whose mind is straighter — theirs, or yours?*
